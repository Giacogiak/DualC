What to try

  • Pick sharp corner and set the resolution low. The dual contour hits the corner exactly; marching squares cuts it off. Now raise the resolution — the chord gets smaller but never disappears.
  • Tick average the normals at seams. Every crossing near the corner now reports the same blended normal, the QEF loses rank, and the corner rounds over. That is exactly what happens on the mesh path when interpolateNormals is left at its default of true.
  • Untick clamp vertices to their cell on the star at low resolution and watch a vertex escape its cell.
  • Sweep the resolution slowly with sharp corner selected. At most settings the deviation sits at machine precision — but at one or two it jumps. Those are the cases where the corner falls almost exactly on a cell boundary, so the neighbouring cell's minimiser lands outside its own cell and gets clamped back in. The C++ does exactly the same thing: clampToBox is applied on both branches of the soft clamp.

The rule being executed

For each cell with at least one sign-changing edge, collect a sample (p, n) per crossing and minimise

E(x) = Σ (ni · (x − pi))²

by the mass-point-shifted normal equations with a truncated eigen pseudo-inverse — the same structure as svd::QefSolver, in two dimensions instead of three.

Then, for every grid edge whose endpoints disagree, join the vertices of the two cells that share it. In 3D that same rule joins the four cells sharing an edge, giving a quad.

What the eigenvalues mean

Try this

Set the configuration to corner, then drag the truncation threshold up past the smaller eigenvalue. The moment it crosses, the solver stops inverting that direction and the solution snaps back to the mass point along it. That is exactly the mechanism that keeps a flat cell well-behaved — and, when the threshold is wrong, the mechanism that loses a sharp feature.

The three outcomes of buildNode

depth >= maxDepthsample the 8 corner signs, populate the 12 edges, allocate HermiteLeafData
depth < minDepthforce a split regardless of geometry — this defends against an L-shaped part whose root-box centre sits in empty space
otherwisesplit iff cellOverlaps(bounds), else become a Hermite-less leaf

Where the adaptivity really comes from

Notice that raising maxDepth refines the whole surface uniformly — flat regions get exactly as many cells as curved ones. There is no error term in the refinement criterion at all.

Now raise simplificationError. The collapse pass merges children back into their parent wherever a single vertex can represent the merged region, guarded by three topology tests. That is where variable resolution is actually produced — and doing it after sampling is cheap, because the QEF error is already available.

Untested. Neither simplifyHermiteOctree nor simplificationError appears anywhere in tests/. It is the largest single coverage hole in the project.

Field algebra: why hard booleans stay sharp

Two primitives, one operator. The value decides the shape; the gradient decides whether the seam comes out as a crease or a fillet.

the composed zero level set gradient at a crossing inside

What you are looking at

The whole trick, in four lines

Vector3 UnionField::gradientAt(const Vector3& p) const {
  // Active operand = the one bounding the union here.
  // Its un-blended gradient is what makes the seam sharp.
  return (a_->valueAt(p) <= b_->valueAt(p))
             ? a_->gradientAt(p)
             : b_->gradientAt(p);
}

Because edgeHit takes the Hermite normal straight from gradientAt, one cell straddling a seam receives two different un-blended normals. Two independent plane constraints intersect at a point, and the QEF puts the vertex there.

Note what this is not. It is not a special case or a corner detector. min is differentiable everywhere except the seam and its derivative is the active operand's, so returning it is the correct subgradient — the sharpness falls out of the field algebra.

The one thing to remember

Two stages, one data structure between them. The sampler is input-aware and contour-agnostic; the contourer is contour-aware and input-agnostic. HermiteOctree is the entire contract.

sampleMeshToHermiteOctree is a three-line adapter that builds a MeshSource and calls sampleFieldToHermiteOctree. So the mesh path is a strict subset of the field path: one refinement algorithm to reason about, one to test, and a new input type never touches the contourer.

The bug to volunteer. dualContourMesh constructs its MeshSource with three arguments where sampleMeshToHermiteOctree passes four — so it silently drops SamplerParams::signMethod. Ask for generalized winding number on triangle soup through the one-call API and you get 3-ray parity instead, with no warning. No test catches it, because every sign-method test goes through the other entry point.

And the things that are genuinely good

  • The two-stage split with one sign-based contract, which makes the mesh path a strict subset of the field path.
  • The active-operand gradient rule: sharp features fall out of the field algebra rather than being detected.
  • The polynomial smin chosen precisely because its ∇h term cancels identically, so the gradient is a plain lerp of the operand gradients — exact, no extra evaluations.
  • Bit-identical output regardless of thread count, by construction rather than by locking.
  • Topological invariant testing: exact Euler characteristic and zero boundary edges on eight procedural meshes, plus byte-identity between the streaming and monolithic export paths.
  • A vendoring policy with teeth — the LGPL octree from the same upstream project was deliberately not copied; the tables and traversal were re-derived to keep the library permissive.
